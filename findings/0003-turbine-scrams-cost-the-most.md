**From 1999 to 2026, the output lost after scrams came to 240 TWh, and turbine and generator scrams account for 78 TWh of it (32%).** A scram's cost here is its *episode*: the unit's days below the power it scrammed from, until it is back within 10 points.

Most episodes are short (a median of 4 days, and 6 for offsite power). The 16 largest are 22% of the total, and the two largest come to 23.6 TWh between them: San Onofre 3's steam generator tube leak in January 2012 (12.8 TWh, and the unit never restarted), and D.C. Cook 1's main turbine fire in September 2008 (10.8 TWh over 430 days, until a refueling outage).

Scram losses fell from 1.08% of net capacity in 1999–2008 to 0.74% in 2016–2025. Turbine and generator losses fell the least (from 0.37% to 0.29%), so they are now 40% of what scrams cost.[^1]

[^1]: An episode counts the output below the pre-scram power whatever cause the later days are booked to, and a refueling outage or the unit's next scram ends it early. 1,638 of the 1,822 unit scrams have one; a scram from under 10% power has none.
